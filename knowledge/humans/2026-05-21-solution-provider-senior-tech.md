## Context

A founder/operator of a technology company building products for seniors. They run sessions at Active Ageing Centres teaching seniors how to use tech, build AI companion products, and pitch nursing homes and old folks' homes. This is the most domain-exposed person we've spoken to — and they have a commercial stake in AI companionship working.

## Notes (raw)

- Typical day: mornings at Active Ageing Centres running senior tech sessions, reviewing progress, facilitating group work. Afternoons pitching organisations — nursing homes, old folks' homes — looking for new angles and markets. Plus investor relations, a pilot collaboration, and "usual tech stuff, conducting sprints."
- Interacts with: internally, product and ops colleagues; externally, seniors, caregivers, and organisations in the space.
- Moment of genuine connection: "almost every engagement session" — elderlies connect to the AI and are willing to share everything, with "strong affection at the start and hyperengaged."
- What loneliness looks like: worst seen is the nursing home — residents "lose the ability to interact with outside world," it's "akin to being a baby (both physical and mental)," caretakers "don't even understand your language," and there's "throwing of temper."
- What stops people admitting it: usually extremes. (1) Strong personalities who "never learnt how to compromise." (2) Over-reliance on one person — "when losing the person, they broke." And elderly especially struggle after losing their "forced structure" — school, work.
- Existing solutions: Active Ageing Centres, elderly programs, fiscal support (contrasts US vs EU models); Singapore tries to sit "between a welfare vs non-welfare model"; "very institutionalised." The shortfall: "implementations fall short of human-element — a caretaker may just be a caretaker but not one that provides care."
- Smallest meaningful change: "someone they can talk/banter with every day — cannot be AI."
- What didn't work: users "figure out certain patterns and it becomes less personal/affectionate." "Even with LLM etc., after many many interactions, they lose the feeling" — though it varies, some after months, some keep engaging for a long time.
- Hardest part of adoption: boredom — "as this is an AI solution, they sooner or later figured it out." Also: "there's no scaled way to reach out to seniors."
- Whitespace: social media catered to seniors; hybrid AI-human communication — described as "memory lane x omegle."

## My read

The most consequential interview of the round. This person has repeated, direct exposure to elderly people using AI companionship — and a financial incentive to believe in it — and they are telling us the affection has a half-life. That deserves serious weight. Also notable: they frame loneliness partly as a structural problem (loss of the "forced structure" of school and work), which lines up with what the elderly interviewees said themselves.

## Follow-ups

Their pilot data, if shareable. What "hybrid AI-human" would concretely mean. The "no scaled way to reach seniors" problem. The language barrier in institutional care.
