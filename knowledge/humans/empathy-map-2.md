The thing that keeps surprising people when they first sit with elderly loneliness research is how badly intuition maps onto it. You expect lonely old people to look lonely — withdrawn, passive, home alone. What you actually find, across six interviews in Singapore in May 2026, is a much stranger picture: people who are surrounded by family and still lonely, people who are out every day and still lonely, people who have tried everything available and are still lonely. The empathy map below is an attempt to compress that strangeness into something a designer or policymaker can hold in one place. It is not a representative sample. It is six people, and the honest thing to do with it is treat it as a set of hypotheses to pressure-test, not as ground truth.

## The six respondents

Worth being explicit about who this is based on before reading anything into it.

A *solution provider* building AI-mediated companionship for elderly users — someone who runs engagement sessions in nursing homes and active ageing centres daily and has watched what happens when elderly people encounter conversational AI for the first time.

A *caregiver* — an adult grandchild who moved a dementia-affected grandmother into their home, managed daily medication, meals, and monitoring, and eventually transitioned the grandmother to an assisted living facility. The lens here is what caregiving looks like from the inside of a family that's doing it without external support.

An *elderly resident*, Indian female, 70s, living with adult children and grandchildren. Retired. No smartphone. Widowed. Still cooking a large pot of curry or dal most days.

An *elderly community visitor*, Chinese male, approximately 70, living near Toa Payoh with his wife. Son and daughter-in-law live close but not in the same flat. Retired. Spends 4–5 hours a day around the Active Ageing Centre and the hawker stall next to it, whether or not a programme is running.

A *frontline care worker* at Lee Ah Mooi Old Age Home — a brief conversation, the worker not particularly open to talking, but enough to get a picture of what the most isolated end of the spectrum looks like: hospital-style beds, old volunteer posters on the wall, interactions mostly confined to nurses.

An *elderly RC Centre-goer* — a sixth respondent added later, filling in a pattern the first five didn't fully cover. Goes to market at 9am, walks at 10am, attends the Resident Committee Centre at 5pm. Has joined every available community activity. Still hits empty days.

## What they say, what they do

The gap between the says and does columns in an empathy map is often where the real insight lives. Here it's striking.

The Indian grandmother never once said she was lonely. What she said was: "I scold the children I live with." "I teach cooking to my grandchildren — it makes me feel more connected." "I would want my children to look after me, but if really needed I can take help from others — but it won't feel natural." Three sentences that, read together, describe a person negotiating the gap between what she expected from family and what she has, using irritability and cooking as the available instruments.

The RC Centre-goer said: "I tell myself to take it as a rest day. But I find myself feeling very sad." And: "What else can I do when I have already tried?" The second sentence is the one worth sitting with. This is not a person who needs to be told about community programmes. This is a person who has exhausted the available supply of community programmes and found it insufficient.

What people *do* tells a different story from what they say they want. The Toa Payoh man goes to the Active Ageing Centre every day, including days with no scheduled activity — he'll sit for four or five hours, eat alone at the hawker stall, then go home. The point isn't the programme. The point is the ambient presence of other people. The grandmother cooks a full pot of food every day as the remaining structuring act of a life that used to have more structure. The dementia grandmother, in her final communicative months, asked only for her son — not for company in general, for a specific named person. The care home residents sit alone in large-ward rooms, with old volunteer posters on the walls and interaction rates that suggest volunteers came a few times and then stopped.

## What they think, what they feel

Two things recur in the thinks column that don't show up in what people say out loud.

The first is something like: *if I ask for help, I've admitted I've failed.* The caregiver described the grandmother as having "a very strong personality" that made it impossible for anyone to offer help. The Toa Payoh man described pride and shyness as barriers — not just his own, but what he observed in the people he saw sitting alone at the centre, who he tried to approach. Strong personality, pride, shyness — these are different words for the same structural problem: the people most isolated are often the ones least able to signal that they need something.

The second is subtler and I think more important: *I'm not sure whoever shows up will stay.* The caregiver's grandmother had been through a few rounds of Homage companionship workers, didn't trust the arrangement, asked to stop. The care home worker described volunteers who "come for a limited period and go away." The solution provider described elderly users who engage intensely with AI companionship at first and then disengage as the novelty fades and the pattern becomes familiar. What's being calibrated in each case is: *is this relationship worth investing in emotionally, given the evidence that it won't last?* The rational answer, given that evidence, is no. And so people don't.

On the feels side: the deepest observation is that the worst moments aren't chronic, they're situational. The RC Centre-goer doesn't describe constant sadness. He describes sadness ambushing him on unscheduled days — when the calendar that he has carefully constructed to keep the loneliness at bay has a gap in it. The Toa Payoh man describes looking forward to the AAC every day, the banter, the games, the recognition — and worrying, separately, about the day when that stops. The grandmother lit up when her granddaughter's face appeared. These are people with functional lives, not broken ones, but the functional lives are load-bearing structures built on top of very little margin. Remove one pillar and the feeling surfaces immediately.

## The pains that the existing system isn't solving

The care home picture is the most legible gap: extremely old, limited mobility, no family visits, volunteers who appeared on the posters but not in the room anymore. That gap is visible and not particularly contested. The more interesting gaps are the ones that aren't immediately legible.

The gap no existing programme addresses cleanly is what I'd call *effort exhaustion* — the person who has joined everything, gone to everything, tried everything, and is still lonely because the supply of meaningful connection runs out before the need does. Community programmes assume a passive or avoidant user. They have no answer for an active user who has lapped the available options. This person doesn't show up as a problem in attendance data because their attendance data looks fine.

Language creates a wall that's easy to miss from the outside. Many of the elderly in this cohort are Mandarin, Hokkien, Cantonese, or Tamil speakers. Even a well-meaning volunteer or an AAC programme facilitator who doesn't share the dialect is, from the resident's perspective, a stranger who can't quite understand them. Loneliness doesn't care that you're technically in a room with other people.

And then there's the pride barrier again — this time as a pain rather than just a think. The people hardest to reach are, systematically, the people least able to be seen as needing help. Any outreach model that relies on self-referral or opting in will under-serve them. The Silver Generation Office's door-to-door model is a direct response to this; the question is whether it has the throughput to find the people the other systems miss.

## What they actually want

The RC Centre-goer's neighbours are people he has known for decades. That's where his primary social anchoring lives — not in a programme, not in a family visit, but in decade-long residential proximity to specific people. The Toa Payoh man doesn't come to the AAC for the activities. He comes for the faces he recognises, the banter with the same people he's been playing cards with for three years. The solution provider said it plainly: what they need is "someone they can banter with every day — cannot be AI."

The thing they want most is also the thing that's structurally hardest to deliver at scale: *a specific relationship that shows up reliably.* Not a programme. Not a service. A person, or something that functions with the consistency and personalisation of a person. The caregiver's summary of what worked in the assisted living transition was precise: "consistency is the most important thing in whatever solution." That's not an insight about any particular product category. It's a constraint on the solution space.

## What confused me / pitfalls

The co-residence number bears repeating because it's the one that most reliably wrongfoots people: more than 80% of elderly Singaporeans who report social disconnection are living *with* family. Not alone. With family. Any intervention that targets people who live alone is targeting a minority of the problem. Any policy success metric that reduces solo-household rates is measuring something that doesn't correlate with what we're trying to fix.

The solution provider's AI observation is worth taking seriously rather than dismissing. Elderly users engaged intensely — "hyper-engaged" was the word — with conversational AI at first. Then engagement tapered. The pattern is: novelty, then recognition that it's a pattern, then disengagement. This isn't a unique property of AI — it's what happens with any relationship substitute that doesn't actually adapt to you the way a person does. The implication isn't that AI companionship doesn't work. It's that *novelty-based engagement is not the same thing as loneliness reduction*, and if you measure the wrong thing you'll get a false positive.
