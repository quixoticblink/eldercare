## Context

A man around 70, interviewed outside an Active Ageing Centre in Toa Payoh. Retired. Lives with his wife; his son and daughter-in-law live close by but not in the same flat. The interviewer also observed a games session at the centre — 30–35 seniors with student volunteers from a nearby school.

## Notes (raw)

- Day: wakes, gets ready to go out. "Doesn't like being at home" — wants to be outside, goes to the coffee shop / hawker centre. If there's an activity at the Active Ageing Centre he likes, he attends; otherwise he "just hangs outside as he at least sees other people there." Has been doing this for years and now knows people to talk to. Spends 4–5 hours a day around the centre and hawker area.
- Around holidays there are activities in the area — joss paper burning, CNY celebrations — that he can be part of.
- Has lunch and sometimes dinner at the coffee shop before going home. On weekends his son visits and they share meals with the daughter-in-law.
- Who he interacts with: his wife (mostly breakfast and dinner together); people around the coffee shop and the Active Ageing Centre.
- Moment of genuine connection: asked what he looks forward to every day — even when there's no activity he joins, he comes and sits at the centre to meet and see people. He looks forward to the games the centre organises; finds joy in them because "activities stimulate his mind, and there is banter / jokes / conversation with fellow players."
- What loneliness looks like: he sees some people "just being there all day without talking to anyone." He doesn't know if they're lonely, but he tries to talk to them. "Not talking to anyone all day is biggest worry for him personally."
- What stops people admitting it / reaching out: language — "many older folks are mandarin speakers only," which makes it hard to connect with non-mandarin speakers, even volunteers. And pride / shyness — "a lot of old folks feel shy reaching out to new people as they don't know them."

## Quotes worth saving

- "Not talking to anyone all day is biggest worry."
- "Doesn't like being at home."
- Connection through games: "banter / jokes / conversation with fellow players."

## My read

He is a model of coping well — he has engineered a daily structure of low-stakes social contact (coffee shop plus the centre) without it being a "programme" he had to be enrolled in. Notably, the Active Ageing Centre works for him even when he doesn't join anything: just being somewhere he can "see other people" is the point. Contrast that with the people he describes who "sit all day without talking." The infrastructure is the same; the outcome isn't. Something about agency and familiarity makes the difference.

## Follow-ups

What separates the AAC-goers who participate from the ones who sit silent. The language barrier — how big is it. Whether "ambient presence" (being somewhere with people, not necessarily interacting) can be designed for.
