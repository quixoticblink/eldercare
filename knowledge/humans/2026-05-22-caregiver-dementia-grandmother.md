## Context

The interviewee is the primary caregiver for their grandmother, who began showing signs of dementia and moved in with them. (Answered the "child with elderly parents" segment — here the carer is the grandchild.)

## Notes (raw)

- Day: grandmother moved in. Getting her to take medicine is very difficult but done every morning. She couldn't even tap in and out of the condo. Interviewee was still working — checked in through the day, made a lunchtime call to confirm food was delivered, came home to feed dinner and medicine after work.
- Who they interact with: "no relatives willing to help — so no people to interact with except for the on-demand caregivers."
- Moment of genuine connection: when grandmother reminisces about her younger days and the interviewee asks about her past — what she was like, who her friends were. And the first moment she meets the interviewee after a period away — "she glows up when she recognises her face."
- What loneliness looks like: "not being able to physically or mentally communicate with others."
- What stops people admitting it: grandmother had a "super strong personality, so nobody wanted to offer help" — and "too much pride to ask."
- Existing solutions: Homage companionship — appreciated by the interviewee but the grandmother didn't appreciate it and asked to stop; the interviewee continued it anyway for safety and to "have an eye."
- Smallest meaningful change: "having someone that can ensure both physical and mental wellness so that people around can be assured."
- Knows the parent's day well — receives daily reports from the assisted living centre.
- Signs of needing connection: never said outright. She "Only asked for her son" (the interviewee's uncle).
- Trust in a solution: conditions "really improved" once she moved into assisted living. "Consistency is the most important in whatever solutions." Comfortable with AI "only because grandma has dementia and probably can't tell between real or AI" — adds that if she could have communicated with her son in her later days "she would have been very happy."

## My read

Two sharp things here. First, the boundary on AI companionship: the caregiver is comfortable with it *only* because the grandmother's dementia means she can't perceive the substitution — implying that for a lucid person, AI wouldn't pass. Second, what the grandmother wanted was not companionship-in-general; it was one specific person, her son. Solutions that offer "someone" may be answering a question she wasn't asking.
