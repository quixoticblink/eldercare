The SGLN leadership team sent us a coaching note — separately from the May 19 Zoom, not part of that meeting — asking us to appoint a devil's advocate. This entry holds the strongest case AGAINST whatever framing we're currently leaning on — right now, [[hmw-current]].

The five seed critiques (drawn from [[hmw-current]]):

1. **Bottleneck is infrastructure, not companionship.** The real bottleneck for elderly community formation in Singapore is HDB layouts, void deck use, senior activity centers, and working-age children's time — none of which a software product can change. We'll build elegant software and nothing will change.

2. **"Daily" is the wrong frequency.** Real human connection runs on natural rhythms — weekly meals, monthly visits, seasonal celebrations. Forcing daily contact may degrade quality rather than enhance it.

3. **"Loneliness" is a Western frame.** In Asian family contexts, what elderly experience is grief about changing family structure (children no longer co-resident, filial duties eroding). Addressing it directly is treating a symptom of a value-shift that's already happened.

4. **Missed population.** Elderly with cognitive decline or dementia don't fit the HMW. "Experience genuine daily connection" works differently for them, or not at all in the way we mean it.

5. **TO is unfalsifiable.** "Brimming with communities" describes any outcome we'd see; no kill criterion. We'll declare success regardless.

## Field evidence — round 1 interviews (2026-05-25)

The first round of interviews ([[../evidence/interview-round-1-findings]]) put real weight behind two of the seed critiques and added one.

Critique 1 (infrastructure / human element) got direct support. The solution provider — who builds eldercare tech for a living — said existing implementations "fall short of human-element," and that the smallest meaningful change "cannot be AI." That is the strongest possible version of the critique, from the least convenient possible source.

A sixth critique, sharpened by the field evidence, now belongs here:

6. **A technology companion has a half-life.** Finding 2 of the round 1 synthesis: AI companionship draws "strong affection at the start" but users "figure out certain patterns" and "lose the feeling" over weeks to months. A caregiver separately said she'd accept an AI companion *only because* her grandmother's dementia made the substitution undetectable. If our eventual solution is a technology companion, it is building on a relationship that is documented to decay. The honest devil's-advocate position: any tech here must route toward human connection, not stand in for it — and if our HMW quietly assumes an app, the HMW is already wrong.

Per the team's call on 2026-05-25, this tension is being *recorded*, not resolved — the HMW in [[hmw-current]] is unchanged for now, and the next HMW iteration should engage critique 6 head-on.

## Partnership evidence — NCSS meeting (2026-06-15)

The 2026-06-15 meeting with NCSS ([[../landscape/ncss]], journal at [[../journal/2026-06-15]]) handed us five more critiques worth arguing seriously. They're less about our HMW being wrong and more about whether the whole *space we're moving toward* — a controlled marketplace for on-demand elderly-adjacent services — survives contact with the evidence.

7. **The deck is exactly the same as one year ago.** NCSS's own frustration was audible; the caregiver-respite HMW they walked us through has been essentially unchanged for over a year of ideation. That is a graveyard signal we should not ignore. Something has kept this stuck — most likely the ownership question in critique 11, second most likely a business case that genuinely doesn't close without subsidy. If we sprint into the same space without diagnosing what stalled everyone else, we will stall the same way.

8. **The matching algorithm is not the moat.** Aditi named it plainly on the call: *"the algorithm from my point of view is not that proprietary."* NCSS agreed. Which raises the actual question we haven't answered: what *is* the moat? Best current guesses are certification, trust, and ecosystem density — none of which are things a three-month sprint can build. If a marketplace-shaped answer wins on operations rather than software, we may be the wrong team for the win condition.

9. **"Well-received" is not a survey.** Vanguard's Community Chaperone and Night Respite pilots ([[../landscape/community-chaperone]], [[../landscape/vanguard]]) are the closest existing operational proof we have of the caregiver-respite model. NCSS's characterisation was "well-received" — no formal outcome survey exists. Any pitch that leans on those pilots as evidence has to carry that caveat honestly, and any decision to scale from them is an act of belief, not measurement.

10. **The awareness gap means demand is latent, not observable.** NCSS's IDIs suggest *over 50%* of the plausible beneficiary population doesn't know existing services exist ([[../evidence/ncss-idi-findings]]). That is convenient for market-sizing but dangerous for demand validation. Any TAM number we extrapolate from current provider volumes is a floor, not a real estimate — and any survey of current caregivers will over-represent the subset already engaged with the system. Our target user may not respond to a survey we can send.

11. **Ownership is unresolved and this is the biggest risk.** NCSS was explicit: they will not own the platform. Aditi drew the line: *"I would not spend our effort over the next three months building something that nobody would take it forward."* Vanguard has interest but not confirmed capacity. A commercial owner is exactly what Grab and Gojek declined to be. If we get to September with a working prototype and no anchoring organisation, we have built a demo, not a solution — and the rubric will grade accordingly.

There is one further critique lurking underneath these five: **NCSS's own consistency finding contradicts the on-demand shape** they and we are both moving toward. Seniors need months to adjust to a new helper and *repel* new faces at first contact. If the marketplace supply is strangers, it fails the same way the AI companion fails. This is closer to a constraint than a critique — flagged for [[../reframing/hmw-2026-06-15-ncss-reframe-signal]] — but it belongs in the devil's-advocate consciousness too.

## Partner over-optimism — added 2026-07-07

Lara delivered this one at the Vanguard-prep meeting ([[../journal/2026-07-07-vanguard-prep]]) and it belongs on the record.

12. **Our partners don't have skin in the game.** NCSS, Vanguard, AIC — the organisations telling us the market is real, the problem is worth solving, the pilot is "well-received." We are the ones investing time, effort, and reputation. They are beneficiaries of anything we build, not co-investors in whether it works. Their enthusiasm is not evidence. Their sizing is not sizing. Their willingness to consult is not partnership. The honest read: they will happily let us build them a business case, and if it works they will take it; if it doesn't they lose nothing. That asymmetry means we should discount everything they say by the amount they're willing to commit *back* — broadcast channels, adoption commitment, budget, or headcount. If the answer to "what will you bring if we build this?" is vague, treat their enthusiasm as noise.

Aditi's productive reframe: *ask them what they'll bring to the table.* Convert wishy-washy interest into actual commitment. That is now a first-class Vanguard question and should stay first-class for every partner conversation from here.

## Vanguard-visit evidence — added 2026-07-08

Two new critiques surfaced (or sharpened) at the 2026-07-08 Vanguard visit ([[../journal/2026-07-08-vanguard-visit]]). Vanguard's honesty in the room — pushing back on our marketplace framing, admitting policy constraints — made these unusually crisp.

13. **The marketplace framing may not fit home care at all.** Angeline's pushback, direct: *"if there is a very strong demand, government will have to fund it. How ministry work is driven by data."* Home care doesn't follow demand-supply logic the way ride-hailing or freelance work does. Demand doesn't reveal itself through pricing — it reveals itself through ministry data collection, and ministry data lags real demand by years. So the whole "marketplace uncovers latent demand" thesis behind our concept has to be re-examined. What we're really building may be an *activation layer + care-planning wrapper + certified-supply orchestrator*, which is technically a marketplace but functionally something else. If that's true, then Snabbit and Urban Company are less useful references than we thought and Vanguard's ICCP integration matters more.

14. **MOH and AIC won't permit preference-matching UX.** The rating and ranking mechanics we sketched in [[../strategy/marketplace-2x2]] run into ministry-level hesitancy. Not because it's technically hard, because it's politically sensitive — care staff have limited ability to be rated by patients, and a formal ranking system creates accountability liability nobody wants. Consequence: whatever matching layer we build cannot include the standard consumer-marketplace UX patterns of stars, reviews, and pick-your-provider. We have to design around this. Assignment-only (like Uber) may be the only politically viable option — but that reduces our differentiation vs the ministry's own internal routing.

There's also the beginning of a counter-argument softening earlier critiques: **critique 11 (ownership) is partially answered.** Vanguard's Angeline explicitly volunteered a Pasir Ris pilot by year-end. Not signed, not scoped, but volunteered — and the honesty of the meeting suggests it's real. If it holds, the "who owns this in October" problem gets a partial answer: a Pasir Ris ICCP pilot is a Vanguard operational scope, not our scope, so we don't need to solve full ownership. We only need to solve the pilot design and the multi-payer stack.

**Critique 12 (partner over-optimism) also softens for Vanguard.** They pushed back on us, admitted data gaps, said out loud *"it's not Vanguard's problem, it's a sector problem."* That is the least optimistic partner posture we've heard. Discount their enthusiasm less than we discounted NCSS's.

## How this entry should grow

Someone on the team owns this role for one sprint. They pick one (or all) of the critiques above and argue it in earnest — not as a thought experiment but as if they actually believe it. The argument lives in this file. Status moves to `draft` when the arguments are written down. Moves to `solid` when the team has actually engaged with them and the HMW has been revised in response (or deliberately kept despite them, with documented reasoning).

The point isn't to win the argument. It's to make sure the HMW survives contact with its strongest opposition.
