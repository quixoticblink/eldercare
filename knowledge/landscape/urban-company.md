Urban Company (formerly UrbanClap) is the canonical example of a **managed marketplace** — [[../strategy/marketplace-2x2]] archetype 4. The team keeps returning to it because it demonstrates that a marketplace can vet, train, price, and standardise its supply without employing them outright. That's roughly the shape we're aiming for.

## What Urban Company does

- Matches customers with vetted service professionals across categories — home cleaning, beauty, appliance repair, plumbing, salon-at-home, spa services.
- **Trains** its professionals — mandatory onboarding modules, standardised service protocols, tool kits provided by the platform.
- **Prices centrally** — customer sees a fixed platform-set price, not the professional's price.
- **Standardises delivery** — service scope, timing, checkout process are all platform-defined.
- **Assigns rather than lists** — customer selects the service, platform assigns the professional.
- Retains customer relationship — repeat bookings are with Urban Company, not with the specific person.

## Why the team keeps citing it

Two reasons. First, it *proves the managed-marketplace pattern works at scale* in a labor category that historically resisted platformisation. Household services in India were fragmented and low-trust before Urban Company; the platform's model normalised booking a plumber the way you'd book a ride. That's the same civilisational shift caregiver-respite would require.

Second, it *doesn't employ the workforce*. The professionals are independent contractors, not W-2 equivalents. This threads the needle on the "gig-vs-employee" question that Aditi flagged as our biggest deferred question in [[../journal/2026-06-25-solutioning]] and [[../journal/2026-07-07-vanguard-prep]]. If Urban Company runs at scale without employing supply, we might be able to too.

## The catch Aditi named

*"If there's not enough demand, and you're running something like a managed marketplace, then you're just bleeding money."*

Urban Company burns money on operational depth — the training, the tool kits, the customer support, the assignment logic. That overhead only pencils out with demand density. In categories or geographies where demand is thin, managed marketplaces fail unit economics even when the model works technically. Our SAM is much smaller than Urban Company's — Singapore vs India, elderly-caregiver-only vs all household services. Whether managed depth is affordable at our scale is the open question.

## What we'd want to learn from them

- **Unit economics.** Per booking, what's the fully-loaded cost of platform overhead (training, ops, dispatch, support)?
- **Break-even density.** How many bookings per neighbourhood per month is the threshold below which managed marketplace can't be sustained?
- **Category-1 vs category-N expansion.** How much cheaper does category N cost to launch once the platform is already running category 1?
- **Repeat vs new-customer economics.** Repeat customers presumably subsidise new-customer acquisition. What's the retention profile?

## Where it might not port

- **Urban Company's supply is professional-skilled** (electricians, beauticians, plumbers) — the profession already exists, they aggregate it. Our supply category (respite caregivers) is *emerging* — most of the labor pool has to be created, not aggregated.
- **Trust bar is very different.** A UC professional in your bathroom doing a manicure is not equivalent to someone alone with your parent for two hours.
- **India labor market vs Singapore.** Very different regulatory and cost structures.
