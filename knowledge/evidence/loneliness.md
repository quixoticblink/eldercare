

Loneliness is defined as a subjective state of perceived social isolation — the distressing experience that occurs when a person's social relationships are perceived as fewer, less satisfying, or less
meaningful than desired.
Source: Hawkley & Cacioppo (2009); widely adopted in academic literature and by the WHO

Loneliness is distinct from social isolation (objective lack of social contact). A person can be surrounded by others yet feel profoundly lonely; conversely, someone living alone may not feel lonely. Both constructs matter for health, but loneliness — being subjective — is harder to detect and address.

Prevalence in Singapore
Multiple studies point to a significant loneliness burden among Singapore's elderly, with rates varying depending on the population studied and the measurement tool used.

Source: Duke-NUS THE SIGNS Study (2016–17); ScienceDirect (2021); PMC (2019)

KEY FINDING: 80%+ of elderly Singaporeans who reported social disconnection were living WITH
family — not alone. Physical co-residence does not prevent loneliness. (NUS/Duke-NUS, 2021)

Global Prevalence
Global estimates vary significantly depending on measurement method, but converge on roughly 1 in 4 to 1 in 3 elderly people experiencing meaningful loneliness.

Expected Trends (Next 5–10 Years)
No authoritative single-point forecast exists for loneliness prevalence in 2030–2035. However, the research consensus is that rates will worsen in the absence of significant intervention, driven by:
• Rapid growth of the elderly population (more people in the highest-risk age bands)
• Rising rates of elderly single-person households globally and in Singapore
• Digital transformation accelerating social exclusion for those who cannot keep up
• Continued erosion of multi-generational household norms, especially in Asian cities
• Lingering post-COVID disruption to social networks and community habits
