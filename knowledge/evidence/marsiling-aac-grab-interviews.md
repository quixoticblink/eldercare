A Grab research team conducted 14 interviews with seniors and caregivers at the Marsiling Active Ageing Centre. The findings were shared verbally in [[../journal/2026-06-25-solutioning]] and slotted onto slide 12 of the Solutioning Brainstorming pptx. This is second-hand evidence — I haven't seen Grab's raw notes or the interview protocol — so treat the numbers as directional. Where they matter to a pitch or the business case, ask Lara for the source Grab document.

## The sample

- **Location:** Marsiling Active Ageing Centre.
- **Total respondents:** 14.
- **Breakdown:** 5 active seniors; 5 partially dependent seniors (PDS); 3 primary caregivers of PDS; 1 secondary caregiver of PDS.
- **Age:** all participants in their late 70s or 80s.

## Marsiling AAC scale (from the centre)

- Approximately **2,000** older adults live in the Marsiling area.
- **1,200** are registered with the centre.
- **~300** regularly visit for activities.

That's a ~15% "regularly engaged" rate against the local elderly population, or ~25% against the registered subset. The gap between *registered* and *regularly visits* is where the "coping vs struggling" persona split from [[../humans/empathy-map-2]] would show up in the data — the 900 who registered but don't come are the ones most likely to be in the pride-gated or effort-exhausted patterns.

## Findings on transport and digital behaviour

Most respondents did not know how to book ride-hailing services. More than half had a smartphone but used it only for calling and texting. They were hesitant to use Grab specifically because it is unfamiliar and they worried about scams or making mistakes when transacting on their phones.

Bus and MRT were the predominant mode of transport. Many avoided taxis because they preferred to trade time for money — taxis felt expensive, and they didn't want to spend on themselves unless necessary. Only two respondents reported using Grab fairly often: one caregiver and one active senior, mainly for longer trips (Marsiling to SGH or Redhill).

When they did take taxis, they typically either flagged one down or asked family or centre staff to book, and paid in cash.

One active senior used social media apps (TikTok, Instagram, Facebook) but did not want to transact on Shopee or Grab because of scam fears. This is interesting — the same person is comfortable consuming content on a smartphone but shuts down at the *transaction* boundary.

Some seniors could not read written Mandarin well; a few mentioned eyesight issues. Language and vision as compound barriers, both under-considered in the design of most consumer apps.

## Takeaways from Grab's own read

Grab's team drew these conclusions, and they're worth carrying forward:

- This segment is **unlikely to adopt ride-hailing as their primary transport** as long as bus and MRT are viable. That means a caregiver-respite marketplace can't lean on the "senior books Grab" pattern for anything routine.
- **Strong use case for medical trips** between home and healthcare institutions — clinic escort is where the elderly *do* need ride-hailing plus a companion.
- **Moderate use case for caregivers booking taxis** to transport fully dependent seniors.
- Caregivers may be *more willing to spend on ride-hailing and food delivery for the seniors in their care* than for themselves. Observed in two of the interviews, needs validation with a bigger sample. This is a hint at where willingness-to-pay actually sits — with the caregiver, not the senior.
- Consider strengthening non-app booking channels (phone, street-hail) to address the digital and language barriers.
- Seniors enjoy AAC activities and afternoon talks. Digital-literacy or engagement sessions run *at the AAC* could be effective — though there's a selection bias, since the 300 who show up are the ones already engaged.

## What this tells us for the respite-marketplace concept

Three practical implications for [[../strategy/respite-marketplace-concept]]:

The senior is not the buyer. If we want the marketplace to be booked, the caregiver has to be the primary user — the Grab data supports this. Design the flow around the caregiver's phone, not the senior's.

Language and vision affordances have to be in the MVP, not v2. Non-Mandarin-literate seniors and reduced-eyesight users are a meaningful slice of the target population. Text-heavy or Mandarin-only interfaces will fail them.

The AAC is a distribution channel, not just a demand pool. Seniors who show up regularly can be recruited into the *supply* side (young-elderly caregivers, Shobhit's Padlet idea) as easily as the demand side. Marsiling's ~300 regularly-visiting seniors is a live pool of potential respite-workers.

## What's thin

Grab-run research targeting Grab-relevant questions. That's fine as directional insight, but it may miss questions we care about — how caregivers currently source respite, willingness-to-pay for specific task categories, whether the "consistency" finding from NCSS's IDIs holds up in the Marsiling sample. If we can get a follow-on interview batch through Vanguard's pool (see [[../journal/2026-07-07-vanguard-prep]]), we should design our own instrument.
