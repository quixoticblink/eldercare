ok so this is second-hand evidence — NCSS ran their own in-depth interviews (IDIs) with caregivers and seniors in late 2025, and shared the findings verbally at our 2026-06-15 meeting. It's important because (a) it's a bigger and more methodological sample than our round 1 fieldwork, (b) it partially corroborates our findings and partially contradicts them, and (c) one of the contradictions is load-bearing enough that any HMW iteration from here has to price it in.

The reason to be careful with this file: I haven't seen the underlying methodology, sample construction, or raw notes. Everything below is what NCSS characterised out loud. Treat it as *what NCSS believes their research shows,* not audited findings. If any specific claim becomes load-bearing to a pitch or a business case, ask them for the source.

## The sample, as far as we know

- **Delivery:** IDIs conducted via a partnership between NCSS, [[care-corner]] (SSA), and SMU (Singapore Management University). Individual interviewer noted only by first name (Brett).
- **Duration:** roughly 20 minutes per interview.
- **Languages:** Chinese, Malay, English. Notable — matched to how the elderly cohort actually speaks at home, which the round 1 findings ([[interview-round-1-findings]]) flagged as under-served.
- **Sample size:** approximately 20–30 respondents. NCSS's phrasing was "about 20 over 30 people" — I'm reading that as 20-something to 30. Small but real.
- **Timeframe:** late 2025.
- **Incentive:** Brett provided incentive tokens (Care Corner delivery vehicle for the participants).

## The findings NCSS shared

### 1. Seniors want *consistency,* not on-demand

This is the finding that most complicates our own thinking. NCSS's exact framing on the call: *"Seniors actually like consistency… when it comes to ad hoc, they actually repel every single time the new person comes because it takes them like months to get used to the person."*

Two things are worth pulling apart in that sentence.

First, the direction — seniors don't just prefer consistency, they *actively push away* new helpers. "Repel" was NCSS's word. That's stronger than "prefer familiar," and it's a design constraint: if the supply layer of any marketplace rotates faces, the demand layer resists.

Second, the timeline — *months* to adjust. Not one visit, not a week. That's a horizon completely out of scale with "on-demand" as a product category.

The tension: NCSS's own HMW is framed around *conveniently accessing respite in a timely manner* — i.e., on-demand. Their own IDIs say seniors reject the on-demand shape at the interface layer. Neither NCSS nor we resolved this in the meeting. Any next HMW iteration ([[../reframing/hmw-2026-06-15-ncss-reframe-signal]]) has to name it. Probably belongs in its own entry — [[consistency-as-design-constraint]].

### 2. Caregiver fatigue and guilt

Caregivers experience emotional and physical fatigue from ongoing caregiving obligations, and this compounds when they are also working. NCSS singled out "working caregivers facing heightened" strain (the transcript cuts off the noun — likely "burden" or "stress"). This matches the sandwich-generation framing our round 1 caregiver interview surfaced ([[../humans/2026-05-22-caregiver-dementia-grandmother]]).

More interesting: **caregivers showed hesitancy in delegating** care duties. The reasons NCSS named: family-duty perceptions, emotional commitment, and reluctance to burden others. External support was sought only when the caregiving demand exceeded personal capacity. **Cost** was a critical decision factor at that point.

This says something specific about the demand side: caregivers won't reach for a respite service *until they're broken.* By then the friction is highest and the discretion is lowest. Any product design that assumes caregivers will proactively use a scheduled respite service is fighting this pattern.

### 3. Seniors value autonomy — help is a last resort

*"Seniors value autonomy and prefer to manage daily tasks independently, engaging support/help when necessary."* This is not new — it matches Signal 03 in [[interview-round-1-findings]] (connection through contribution, not through being cared for). But it stacks with Finding 2 to create the compound pattern that both sides of the transaction (senior and caregiver) resist help until they can't refuse it.

### 4. Awareness gap: over 50% of potential beneficiaries don't know existing services exist

Directional, not sourced. NCSS's exact framing: *"More than 50% of people don't even… are not aware of such services."* Treat as a hypothesis worth verifying in our own IDIs rather than a citable statistic.

If it holds, it says demand is *latent, not observable.* Which means any market-sizing exercise using current service uptake will systematically understate the addressable population. Which is convenient for our business case — and also a warning to be honest about the extrapolation logic if we use the number.

### 5. Existing market services take 2–3 weeks

NCSS validated this themselves — they dialled in and emailed the two named commercial providers ([[homeage]], [[jagami]]) and the confirmation lead-time was 2–3 weeks. That is the specific gap the on-demand pitch sits in. Solid finding — verifiable by anyone who wants to replicate.

### 6. The population sweet spot: "unhealthy 60s, healthy 80s"

Aditi coined this framing in-meeting and NCSS agreed. The IDIs suggest the productive target is seniors who need support but are still ambulatory — the "unhealthy 60s" who've had a health event but can still leave the house, and the "healthy 80s" whose age is starting to compound but haven't lost mobility. Neither the very-well 60s nor the bedridden 80s+ are the right first population.

This matches [[../humans/empathy-map-2]]'s three-persona split — the coping-elderly, the surrounded-but-still-alone, and the tried-everything-and-still-lonely — none of whom are at the frailty edge.

## Where it contradicts our round 1 findings

- **Finding 1 (consistency) contradicts our assumption that on-demand is the wedge.** Round 1 didn't test that assumption directly; it's implicit in the "genuine daily connection" framing. NCSS's research explicitly tested it and found the on-demand shape triggers rejection.
- **Finding 4 (awareness gap) softens our round 1 sense that the coping-elderly have "tried everything."** They've tried everything they *know about.* There is a whole tier of services below the visibility threshold.

## Where it corroborates

- **Finding 2 corroborates our caregiver interview** — hesitancy to delegate, seek help only when overwhelmed.
- **Finding 3 corroborates our Signal 03** — autonomy over receipt of care.
- **Finding 6 corroborates our empathy-map segmentation** — the productive persona sits in the middle band.

## What confused us / pitfalls

I do not have the underlying instrument. NCSS did not walk through the interview protocol or the coding methodology. I would not put any of these numbers in a pitch deck without asking them for the source document.

The consistency finding especially deserves care. "Months to adjust" is a strong specific claim. If it's directional from a handful of interviews, that's very different from something structured against a scale. Ask.

The >50% awareness gap is the most likely to be misused. It's the kind of statistic that spreads quickly and gets stripped of its "directional / no citation" caveat. If we quote it publicly we should either verify or attribute it explicitly to NCSS's in-meeting characterisation.
