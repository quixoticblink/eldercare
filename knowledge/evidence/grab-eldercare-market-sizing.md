Lara shared a set of Grab internal slides during the [[../journal/2026-06-25-solutioning]] session that sized the eldercare opportunity in Singapore from Grab's perspective. Everything below is either directly from Grab's internal analysis or from the public sources their analysis cites (Duke-NUS, MOH, Straits Times, SingStat, IMDA). This means we can quote it — the numbers are Grab's synthesis of public data, not proprietary intelligence. That's what Lara confirmed in the session.

Standard evidence caveat: partner-sourced sizing is orientation, not evidence. Shobhit was firm on this — *"getting any kind of secondary data based on any research will be quite weak."* His counterproposal is to work top-down independently and check convergence. This entry preserves what Grab said; a separate top-down exercise still owes us numbers.

## The headline numbers

- **~$425M** addressable market for Grab-adjacent eldercare services in Singapore (SAM — the portion of the TAM Grab could realistically serve).
- **~290,800** seniors currently live without their children (Grab's target demographic for food and mart services).
- **~9.75% CAGR** in the elderly demographic over the next 5 years.
- **>20%** of Singapore's population will be 65+ from 2026 — Singapore hits *"super-aged"* status.
- **24%** of seniors currently digitally capable (Grab's framing — meaning "comfortable using emerging technologies"). Expected to grow naturally.

## Customer segmentation (Grab's slide 16)

Grab breaks the eldercare landscape into a supply-and-demand grid:

**Demand side — the seniors, by ADL dependency:**

- **Independent seniors** — no ADL assistance needed. Base: **>800,000** in Singapore (per Duke-NUS, 2030 projection). Of these, ~83,000 live alone (SingStat 2024).
- **Partially dependent seniors** — need help with 1–2 ADLs but not too ill for nursing homes. ~44,000.
- **Fully dependent seniors** — need help with ≥3 ADLs. ~39,000. Out of scope for Grab's framing — they need clinical care, not marketplace services.

**Supply side — the caregivers:**

- **Primary (informal) caregivers** — family members, day-to-day. **>27,000**.
- **Professional caregivers / helpers** — trained, paid, home or facility. **>12,400**.
- **Respite / interim caregivers** — temporary, professional or volunteer. **>2,400**. This is the group our platform is trying to grow.

*ADL dependency is measured by the Katz Index of Independence — six tasks: bathing, dressing, toileting, transferring, continence, feeding.*

## The $425M TAM breakdown methodology

Slide 17 shows how Grab arrived at the $425M number. The method:

- **Users** — based on demographic data, focused on high-usage customers relevant to each service.
- **Frequency of purchase** — modelled from estimated service usage per user category.
- **Average spend per purchase** — pulled from Grab historical AOVs, AIC per-trip budgets, and competitor pricing benchmarks.

This is a bottom-up build from three known inputs. It's a defensible number but it's *anchored on Grab's existing service categories* — mobility, food delivery, mart, and adjacent — not on caregiver-respite specifically. For our purposes it's a *ceiling* on the Grab-shaped opportunity, not a floor on the respite marketplace.

## The 290,800 living-without-children segment (slide 18)

Grab's Segment-2 focus: seniors who could be reached via food delivery and mart services.

- **Base: 290,800** seniors living without their children.
- **Digital penetration: 24%** — Grab's assumption for the currently reachable subset.
- **Food delivery frequency assumption:** 52 orders per year (~1/week) — to be validated in discovery.
- **Mart delivery frequency assumption:** 6 orders per year (~1 every other month) — driven by auto-restocks and bulky/heavy items.

The 24% "digitally capable" number is the load-bearing assumption in the whole sizing. If it's wrong the market shrinks proportionally. Marsiling AAC findings ([[marsiling-aac-grab-interviews]]) suggest it may be *lower* than 24% for seniors 75+ specifically — most have smartphones but use them only for calling and texting.

## Related eldercare-relevant government initiatives (slide 15)

- **Age Well SG** — $3.5B committed over next decade to encourage active living and expand Active Ageing Centres from 154 to >223 by 2025.
- **Enhanced Long-Term Care Subsidies and Grants**.
- **Senior's Mobility and Enabling Fund** — $300M to ease access to home and community-based care; and ~$50M annually over 5 years for mobility aid subsidies.

These are policy-level tailwinds — they don't directly size demand, but they shape the funding and partnership environment we're pitching into.

## What this data does well

- Gives us a defensible upper-bound number ($425M) for a pitch.
- Segments the population in a way that maps cleanly to what we'd charge and to whom.
- Anchors the "super-aged from 2026" framing in a specific sizing number.
- Frames government spending as a partnership backdrop.

## What this data doesn't do well

- **Doesn't size caregiver-respite specifically.** The 290,800 living-without-children is a *food and mart* target; not all of them have active caregivers, and not all caregivers have respite demand. The number we care about is a subset.
- **Doesn't validate willingness-to-pay.** Marsiling findings hint that seniors trade time for money — the marketplace has to price for the caregiver's willingness, not the senior's.
- **Depends on a 24% digital capability rate** that field evidence suggests may be optimistic for the target age band.
- **Doesn't reflect the awareness gap.** NCSS's >50% unaware finding ([[ncss-idi-findings]]) means observable current spend understates latent demand — but by how much is speculation.
