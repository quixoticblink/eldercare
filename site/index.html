The Question

One sentence, hard-earned.

How Might We is a design-thinking scaffold for framing a problem so that it invites ideas without prescribing answers. The form is How might we help [WHO] [DO] so that [TO]. The trick is that each of those three slots is doing real work — getting any of them wrong sends the rest of the project somewhere it shouldn't go.

How we got here

Each of us wrote our own HMW first — six attempts in total. The exercise generated different WHO narrowings (couples staying alone, elderly without kids, socially isolated, lonely, comprehensive), different DO formulations (feel less lonely, feel connected, age with family, genuine connection, meaningful companionship, sense of belonging), and different TO scales (individual happiness, healthcare burden reduction, healthy ageing, societal flourishing).

Two cells did most of the work for the synthesis. The DO came from Zheng Wei's first attempt — "experience genuine daily connection." It names quality (genuine) and rhythm (daily) together, and it's a positive description of what people actually want rather than a negation of what they don't. The TO came from Lara — "Singapore brimming with elderly communities that are happy, mentally/physically healthy." It pulls the outcome from individual to societal scale; the other TOs we wrote fit inside one life. The WHO stayed general on purpose — narrowing it before we'd talked to anyone would have baked in an assumption.

Honest about what this doesn't do well. "Elderly in Singapore" is ~1.1M people and they don't have one shape — the empathy maps will tell us how to narrow. "Genuine" is hard to operationalize without becoming a feel-good word we wave at any solution we like. The leap from individual daily connection to societal flourishing is large and has at least one missing middle step (a theory of how communities form, not just how individuals connect). "Brimming" is rhetorical, not measurable — we owe ourselves a metric eventually. And the HMW invites the devil's-advocate critique that companionship isn't the real bottleneck at all — infrastructure and time are. Someone on the team is taking that argument up for one sprint.

Full synthesis with the per-row reasoning, devil's advocate seeds, and open questions is in the wiki: knowledge/reframing/hmw-current.md. The verbatim record of the six original HMWs is at knowledge/reframing/hmw-2026-05-19-individual-exercise.md.

The Problem Space

It looks like social services. It is mostly demographics.

Eldercare looks like a social-services problem from the outside — old people need help; you build homes and hire caregivers and set up subsidies. The moment you sit with it for a few hours, you realize it's a demographics-meets-technology problem wearing a social-services costume. The world's old-age dependency ratio is doing something it has literally never done before, and most existing eldercare infrastructure was designed for the previous demographic shape: lots of young adults per elderly person, big multi-generational families, somebody at home during the day. Those preconditions are breaking simultaneously.

Singapore is a useful place to look at the problem because it concentrates. Population ~5.9M. Roughly 19% are 65+ today, around 1.1 million people — projected to be ~25% by 2030. Of those, somewhere around 70,000 live alone, and surveys keep finding that 30–50% of community-dwelling elderly report feeling lonely "often" or "sometimes." Round hard: on the order of 30,000 people in one small city-state who would tell you on a survey they're lonely most of the time. Roughly the capacity of the National Stadium. The funnel matters because it tells you the problem isn't diffuse and abstract — there's an identifiable subpopulation and you can probably name them by postal code.

Inside the broader topic, eldercare factors into at least four functions that the old family system used to bundle: physical care (ADLs), instrumental support (logistics, transport, bills), companionship (someone present, mutual existence), and cognitive/identity work (someone who remembers your stories). Most products pick one and pretend the others don't exist. Companionship is where the team has put its bet — partly because the unit economics of human companionship are catastrophic and partly because it generalizes globally in a way physical care doesn't.

The full observational entry — including what confused us, what's folklore vs. what we've verified, and the structural reasons companionship can't be cleanly isolated from instrumental support — lives in the wiki: knowledge/eldercare.md.

What We Heard

Six conversations with the people the question is about.

Between 21 and 25 May we ran our first round of field interviews — a solution provider, a frontline caretaker, two children of elderly parents, and elderly people themselves.

Six is a small number, and we're honest about that — a first round, reaching whoever we could reach quickly. It can't tell us how common anything is. But six good conversations surface mechanisms, and these five signals changed how we think about the question.

01

Loneliness is an empty calendar.

The clearest definition came unprompted, from an elderly person with an otherwise full, well-organised life. Loneliness wasn't the absence of people — it was the absence of a reason for the day to have a shape. The elderly who cope best are the ones who have rebuilt their own daily structure.

"Loneliness is when I wake up without any activities planned for the day."— Elderly resident, interviewed 25 May
02

AI companionship has a half-life.

This came from the person with the most reason to tell us the opposite — the founder of a company that builds AI products for seniors. The affection is real at first; then users detect the patterns and the feeling fades. Asked what the smallest meaningful change would be, they said it "cannot be AI."

"Even with LLM, after many many interactions, they lose the feeling."— Founder of a senior-technology company
03

Connection flows through contribution.

An elderly woman living in a full multi-generational house — who, in the same breath, said she "eats alone" — described the thing that actually made her feel connected. It wasn't being cared for. It was having something to teach.

"I teach cooking to my grandchildren, and it makes me feel more connected."— Elderly woman living with family
04

Consistency is the product.

Across a caregiver, a frontline caretaker, and the solution provider, the same failure mode: help that arrives and then leaves. Volunteer programmes, paid visits, even product engagement all decay. Durability is the thing being asked for — and charity-shaped help fails even when it's durable.

Volunteers "do for a limited period and go away."— Caretaker at an old age home
05

Family is the answer people want — and can't get.

Every solution competes with a specific, named, irreplaceable person. The grandmother with dementia "only asked for her son." The realistic question isn't how to replace family — it's what the best non-family option looks like, and how to keep it from feeling like a consolation prize.

"It's part of life. Children only come visit once in a while. Nothing more to be done."— Elderly resident, interviewed 25 May
