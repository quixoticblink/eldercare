Our Journey

This is a working public record.

We started on May 19, 2026 on a Zoom call. Five of us. We didn’t walk in knowing what the problem was — we walked in with our own family stories and a willingness to listen for what they had in common. The question has moved twice since. This page updates as the sprint moves forward. Old entries stay so the journey is visible.

May 19, 2026 · Day 1

From open brainstorm to one HMW

First Zoom. We started with open brainstorming — what problems we cared about, what was happening in our own lives. Then we ran desktop research in parallel, each pulling in stats, prior art, founder stories from our networks. We surfaced roughly eleven distinct angles: reminiscence therapy, caregiver support, digital inclusion, voice accessibility, mobility and safety, and more. As we compared them against our own family situations, companionship and loneliness kept coming up as the unifying word. Five different families, same underlying pattern. We then ran the How Might We exercise: each of us wrote our own WHO / DO / TO statement. Six attempts. We synthesized them into one.

July 11, 2026 · Broadening

Vanguard said crisis; the team reframed to need

The Jul 8 HMW mirrored Vanguard’s own framing — Angeline had named crisis as the sharpest unmet pain, and we wrote her six triggers straight into the sentence. Three days later, as a team, we caught that adoption as too tight for the product we actually want to build. August 3, 2026 · NCSS & Vanguard

Told to run a tabletop, not a launch

We came asking for a live pilot before September. Vanguard said no, and then offered something better than a polite no — a tabletop exercise: five sets of senior, micro-jobber and coordinator in one room for an afternoon, walking every workflow in what they called “a very safe environment, rather than directly rolling it out already and having all the hiccups.” Two sessions, one arranged by Vanguard and one by NCSS and Care Corner, targeting the week of 17 August. We took it immediately, and not as a concession: seven weeks of partner meetings have produced agreement, and agreement is not evidence. What we want from a room full of people is the resistance.

The harder truths came with it. Supply is the binding constraint, not demand — certification gates who can serve, and a booking promise the operator cannot honour is a harm to a vulnerable person, not a bad user experience. And Vanguard reframed success as “whether it has helped to lessen your administrative burden” — an operator-efficiency measure that quietly moves the primary user from the caregiver to the coordinator. Caregiver relief, resilient caregiving, and operational digitisation are three different products, and this meeting left all three on the table.

How the question moved

We started on May 19 with the elderly at the centre of the WHO and genuine daily connection as the DO. That framing survived the round-one field interviews. It didn’t survive the June meeting with NCSS, which had held a sharper problem statement — caregiver respite — for over a year, backed by their own IDI research. Two weeks later, on June 25, we made the reframe official: caregivers become the WHO; companionship nests inside as one of six respite task categories. On July 8, sitting in Vanguard’s Hougang care home (the site of one of their existing operations; the pilot estate they’re planning for the eldercare prototype is in Pasir Ris), we adopted Vanguard’s own framing of the sharpest unmet pain: crisis. Angeline named six triggers, and we wrote them into the HMW the same afternoon. Three days later, on July 11, as a team we caught our adoption of Vanguard’s framing as too tight for the product we actually want to build. The DO isn’t only crisis — it’s any moment when a caregiver’s own life competes with elder care. A work meeting overlapping a medical appointment. A vacation. A hospitalisation. Vanguard said crisis; the team reframed to need arises. Same product, wider demand curve.

“When a need arises,” not when a crisis hits. Vanguard said crisis; the team reframed to need. Vanguard-anchored, ICCP-layered, still year-end.

Honest about what this doesn’t do well. “When a need arises” trades the sharpness of the Jul 8 crisis-only framing for something closer to how caregivers actually live — but the tradeoff is now two demand curves in one product (planned, low-urgency respite AND urgent, high-stakes activation), and they may want different UX, different SLAs, different prices. We’re also still narrowed to a subpopulation Vanguard’s current pipeline can reach — Pasir Ris ICCP participants — with no evidence yet that the design generalises. And the reframe still leaves the original societal outcome behind — happy, healthy elderly communities was rhetoric we’ll need to earn back once the pilot has data. Devil’s advocate is active on all of it.

The Problem Space

It looks like social services. It is mostly demographics.

Eldercare looks like a social-services problem from the outside — old people need help; you build homes and hire caregivers and set up subsidies. The moment you sit with it for a few hours, you realize it’s a demographics-meets-technology problem wearing a social-services costume. The world’s old-age dependency ratio is doing something it has literally never done before, and most existing eldercare infrastructure was designed for the previous demographic shape: lots of young adults per elderly person, big multi-generational families, somebody at home during the day. Those preconditions are breaking simultaneously.

Singapore is a useful place to look at the problem because it concentrates. Population ~5.9M. Roughly 19% are 65+ today, around 1.1 million people — projected to be ~25% by 2030. Of those, somewhere around 70,000 live alone, and surveys keep finding that 30–50% of community-dwelling elderly report feeling lonely “often” or “sometimes.” Round hard: on the order of 30,000 people in one small city-state who would tell you on a survey they’re lonely most of the time. Roughly the capacity of the National Stadium. The funnel matters because it tells you the problem isn’t diffuse and abstract — there’s an identifiable subpopulation and you can probably name them by postal code.

Inside the broader topic, eldercare factors into at least four functions that the old family system used to bundle: physical care (ADLs), instrumental support (logistics, transport, bills), companionship (someone present, mutual existence), and cognitive/identity work (someone who remembers your stories). Most products pick one and pretend the others don’t exist. Our first bet was companionship. Two months of field work moved us one layer up: the household’s primary caregiver — the person holding all four functions together — is the one whose failure mode is most acute, most under-served, and most legible as a paying customer. Companionship remains inside the tent; it just isn’t the tent.

The full observational entry — including what confused us, what’s folklore vs. what we’ve verified, and the structural reasons companionship can’t be cleanly isolated from instrumental support — lives in the wiki: knowledge/eldercare.md.

What We Heard

Six conversations with the people the question is about.

Between 21 and 25 May we ran our first round of field interviews — a solution provider, a frontline caretaker, two children of elderly parents, and elderly people themselves.

Six is a small number, and we’re honest about that — a first round, reaching whoever we could reach quickly. It can’t tell us how common anything is. But six good conversations surface mechanisms, and these five signals changed how we think about the question. They’ve since been joined by NCSS’s in-depth-interview findings and a small round at Marsiling AAC — both linked below.

01

Loneliness is an empty calendar.

The clearest definition came unprompted, from an elderly person with an otherwise full, well-organised life. Loneliness wasn’t the absence of people — it was the absence of a reason for the day to have a shape. The elderly who cope best are the ones who have rebuilt their own daily structure.

“Loneliness is when I wake up without any activities planned for the day.”— Elderly resident, interviewed 25 May
02

AI companionship has a half-life.

This came from the person with the most reason to tell us the opposite — the founder of a company that builds AI products for seniors. The affection is real at first; then users detect the patterns and the feeling fades. Asked what the smallest meaningful change would be, they said it “cannot be AI.”

“Even with LLM, after many many interactions, they lose the feeling.”— Founder of a senior-technology company
03

Connection flows through contribution.

An elderly woman living in a full multi-generational house — who, in the same breath, said she “eats alone” — described the thing that actually made her feel connected. It wasn’t being cared for. It was having something to teach.

“I teach cooking to my grandchildren, and it makes me feel more connected.”— Elderly woman living with family
04

Consistency is the product.

Across a caregiver, a frontline caretaker, and the solution provider, the same failure mode: help that arrives and then leaves. Volunteer programmes, paid visits, even product engagement all decay. Durability is the thing being asked for — and charity-shaped help fails even when it’s durable. This finding survived first contact with NCSS’s own IDI research, and has since hardened into a load-bearing design constraint (see Strategy below).

Volunteers “do for a limited period and go away.”— Caretaker at an old age home
05

Family is the answer people want — and can’t get.

Every solution competes with a specific, named, irreplaceable person. The grandmother with dementia “only asked for her son.” The realistic question isn’t how to replace family — it’s what the best non-family option looks like, and how to keep it from feeling like a consolation prize.

“It’s part of life. Children only come visit once in a while. Nothing more to be done.”— Elderly resident, interviewed 25 May

Empathy Map

Lonely elderly rarely look lonely.

The customer empathy map, synthesized from all six field interviews — the elderly person at the centre of the original question, in their own words and behaviours.

You expect a lonely old person to look the part: withdrawn, passive, home alone. The interviews kept showing the opposite — people surrounded by family who still feel alone, people out of the house every day who still feel alone, people who have joined every programme on offer and still hit empty days. The map below compresses that into one view. It is six people, not a representative sample — treat it as hypotheses to pressure-test, not as settled truth. As of July, the WHO the sprint is designing for has shifted to the caregiver alongside this person; the empathy work for that new WHO is in progress and lives in knowledge/humans/.

Feels
  • Sadness that ambushes on the unscheduled, empty days.
  • A quiet worry about the day the routine stops.

Pains
  • Empty days, after doing everything they were told to do.
  • A full house — and still eating alone.
  • Children who visit only once in a while.

Gains
  • A day with a repeatable shape.
  • Low-stakes third places — the coffee shop, the hawker centre, the void deck.
  • A role: something to teach, to contribute, to be needed for.
  • Decades-old neighbours — familiarity, not stranger-matching.
  • Consistency: a relationship that reliably shows up.
The throughline. Across all six interviews, what people want is not a programme or a service — it is a specific relationship that shows up reliably. “Consistency is the most important thing in whatever solution,” as one caregiver put it. That is less a feature request than a constraint: it rules out most transient, programmatic, or novelty-driven answers. It later became the load-bearing design constraint for the strategy.

Strategy

A need-activated marketplace, layered onto a real operator.

Somewhere between the NCSS meeting in June and the days after the Vanguard site visit in July, the problem shifted — from “how do elderly people experience daily connection” to “how does trusted help show up when a caregiver needs it — a work meeting, a vacation, a hospitalisation.” Same population, different unit of the problem — and a much sharper thing to build against.

The working shape is a curated, certified caregiver-respite marketplace: managed enough to earn trust, on-demand enough to matter when the need is urgent, planable enough to book two weeks out. Not Fiverr (too open), not Uber (supply isn’t fungible when the customer is an 82-year-old). Closer to Urban Company, with the “bleeding money at thin demand” caution we picked up along the way. Six task categories — from companionship through personal care through household errands — each with its own certification profile and price. The pilot doesn’t try to launch a marketplace from scratch; it inserts a respite-activation layer into an operator (Vanguard) that already has the supply, the trust, and the SOP. Vanguard’s crisis triggers gave the pilot its initial wedge — they’re where willingness-to-pay is clearest — but the team’s Jul 11 reframe added planned respite (the office meeting, the vacation, the appointment) alongside them from day one: same supply layer, same operator, wider demand curve.

The shape

Managed marketplace

A 2×2 of matching-control × management-depth places us top-right — platform assigns, platform manages the supply. Rules out Fiverr-style open bidding and Uber-style dispatch. Six task categories nest inside one platform, each with its own certification profile.

strategy/marketplace-2x2.md

The constraint

Consistency is load-bearing

NCSS’s own IDI research says seniors need months to adjust to a new helper — and actively repel strangers at first contact. A marketplace that rotates faces fails at appointment one. Vanguard’s operational answer: phone triage → first session with staff → subsequent sessions with a micro-worker who now reads as a Vanguard extension.

strategy/consistency-as-design-constraint.md

The stack

Multi-payer, one supply layer

Willingness-to-pay is broken at the senior-facing layer — sessions cost ~$26 to deliver and many clients can’t pay the $25–35 tag. The stack: private pay at the top, healthcare fund subsidy, philanthropic capital for lower-income access, and corporate partners (hotels donating low-season rooms as respite venues, employer benefits). No single payer works.

evidence/vanguard-operational-data.md

Vanguard, in one paragraph. Vanguard is the MOH Holdings unit that runs 5 nursing homes, four active pilots, and Singapore’s only ministry-owned home-care operation. Chaperone volume is real but small — 20–30 bookings a month across four centres. What they named as the biggest unmet pain was crisis activation: FDW runaways, spousal-couple hospitalisations, post-discharge scrambles, sudden decline, grief after spouse death, caregiver’s own crisis. The Jul 11 broadening puts planned respite on top of that — a caregiver’s office meeting, a two-week vacation, a routine outing — same supply layer, same operator, longer lead-time demand. On July 8 they offered a Pasir Ris pilot by year-end, layered onto the existing ICCP platform. It’s the closest thing to skin-in-the-game we’ve been offered.

The Build

The app is live. The deck is a draft.

What started as eighteen clickable screens now runs at a real address, with real sign-in codes going to real phones.

The working name is Kakis — Singlish for the trusted companions you can call on — and it is provisional until the team votes. It walks all three sides of the loop: a caregiver activating respite, planned or urgent; a respite giver training through Vanguard’s real certification modules; and an ops console where one coordinator handles exceptions instead of rosters. Every screen traces back to the evidence.

Three journeys, as they actually work

The caregiver sets up a household and a care plan once — medications, mobility, languages, emergency contacts. Booking is service, then urgency, then what happened: urgent and soon bookings ask for the trigger, which is what turns a booking into a story the coordinator can prioritise. When the kaki arrives, the caregiver’s screen shows a four-digit start code to read aloud. Afterwards: a report, and a private note the care team sees but the kaki does not.

The kaki declares when they can work as a Monday-to-Sunday grid of half-days, plus dated exceptions — because most respite givers are employed elsewhere and “Tuesdays and Saturday mornings, except I’m away next week” is the shape of a real life. They accept an assigned visit or pass it straight back, start it by entering the family’s code, and finish with a short report. They never see that code in their own app. That single asymmetry is what turns “the app says a visit happened” into “someone was at the door and the family let them in.”

The coordinator approves people and matches visits, urgent first. Each candidate carries an availability badge for that exact date and half-day, history with this senior, language and service match, and current workload — sorted best-first but never filtered, because an urgent case may still justify phoning someone nominally off. Approval and matching can both be automated; all three switches default to off, because each one removes a human from a decision about who enters a vulnerable person’s home.

Money is deliberately honest. Every rate, hour and subsidy percentage sits in one file with its source stated, most of it still marked placeholder — the community care subsidy and foundation top-up are illustrative, not confirmed. Every screen showing a dollar figure says so.

What we’re being graded on

The sprint has a four-part rubric, 25 points each. The wiki structure is shaped by it.

25 pts

Leadership Journey

Problem ownership and conviction. Evidence of learning, adaptation, resilience over six months. Quality of collaboration across diverse backgrounds. Fed by journal/.

25 pts

Quality of Thinking

Relevance to Singapore’s tech ecosystem. Clarity of strategic pathway and roadmap. Evidence-backed analysis and insights. Fed by problem/, evidence/, landscape/, strategy/.

25 pts

MVP / Prototype

Human centricity (not just slides). Level of prototyping: tool, dashboard, workflow, simulation. Feasibility, viability, desirability. Fed by humans/ + later prototype/.

25 pts

Presentation & Influence

Clarity, structure, compelling storytelling. Executive presence. Strength of pitch — convinces, not just informs. Fed by later pitch/.

Eldercare Sprint

A six-month leadership sprint working out how caregivers of the relatively healthy elderly in Singapore can activate trusted respite when a need arises — planned or urgent. Started May 19, 2026. Five people. Public journey. Anchored on Vanguard’s Pasir Ris pilot from July 8 onward.
